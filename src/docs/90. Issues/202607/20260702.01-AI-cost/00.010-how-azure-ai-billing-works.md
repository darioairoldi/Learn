## 🎯 Why this matters for the cost platform

The [AI cost platform](00.000-overview.md) treats cloud billing as the financial source of truth.  
To reconcile and attribute AI spend correctly, you first need to know *how each Azure AI service turns usage into a billed line item*. This article is the Azure-first deep dive behind the platform's `fact_cloud_cost` table.

It focuses on the two building blocks of most enterprise AI streams:

-   <mark>Foundry models</mark> — the generative models (chat, embeddings, image, audio) you deploy and call through Microsoft Foundry.
-   <mark>Azure AI Search</mark> — the retrieval engine behind retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and agentic retrieval, in all its billing flavors.

Non-generative AI services (<mark>Vision</mark>, <mark>Document Intelligence</mark>, <mark>Speech</mark>, and similar) are covered separately in [how other Azure AI billing works](00.030-how-azure-ai-other-billing-works.md).

## 🧭 Two billing truths recap

Keep the platform's core principle in mind while reading: cloud billing splits cost by *<mark>**infrastructure boundaries**</mark>* (<mark>subscription</mark>, <mark>resource group</mark>, <mark>resource</mark>, <mark>meter</mark>), never by user or request type. Every meter in this article lands in billing as an infrastructure fact. The *who* and *why* must come from <mark>gateway and application telemetry</mark>, then get reconciled against these meters.

So as you read each billing unit below, ask two questions:

1.  What is the <mark>**billed unit**</mark> (token, hour, query, transaction)?
2.  What telemetry would let me attribute that unit to a user, team, or request type?

## 🧠 How Foundry models are metered

Foundry language and vision models process input by breaking it into <mark>**tokens**</mark> (units of text roughly four characters long for typical English). Text, image, and audio workloads can all use token-based metering, though the billing unit and rate vary by model, deployment type, and meter.

Token cost applies to <mark>both directions</mark>:

-   <mark>**Input tokens**</mark> — everything you send (system prompt, context, user message, tool definitions).
-   <mark>**Output tokens**</mark> — everything the model generates.
-   <mark>**Cached tokens**</mark> — repeated input prefixes served from a temporary cache, billed at a discount (more below).

For most models, pricing is now <mark>**quoted per 1 million tokens**</mark>.  
A single chat completion is charged for its input tokens plus its output tokens. Output tokens usually cost more than input tokens because generation is more compute-intensive.

> Rule of thumb for estimation: a request that sends 1,000 tokens and receives 1,000 tokens is billed for 2,000 tokens, split across the input and output rates for that specific model.

### Models sold by Azure versus partner models

Two billing families sit behind Foundry, and they land on your bill differently:

-   <mark>Models sold by Azure</mark> (Azure OpenAI GPT and o-series, and other Azure-direct models) bill on <mark>first-party token meters</mark>. You see per-model input/output meters directly in Cost Management.
-   <mark>Models from partners</mark> (for example Anthropic Claude) are sold and operated by the provider and require an <mark>Azure Marketplace</mark> subscription. Claude bills through <mark>Claude Consumption Units (CCU)</mark>: your tokens are rated in USD at Anthropic's published per-model rates, converted to CCU at a fixed **$0.01 per CCU** (100 CCU = $1.00), and reported to Marketplace hourly. Your Azure bill shows a <mark>single CCU line item</mark> — not per-model token meters — while the per-model token breakdown stays in the Foundry portal's Monitoring tab. CCU is MACC-eligible.

This distinction matters for the platform: Claude cost arrives as one Marketplace/CCU meter in `fact_cloud_cost`, so per-model and per-user attribution must come from Foundry telemetry, and you reconcile the CCU total rather than per-model token meters. See the [appendix](#-appendix-current-cost-reference-usd) for current Claude rates.

## 🏭 Deployment types and their billing

The deployment type you choose determines *how* a model is billed, independently of *which* model you run. Foundry offers these types for models sold by Azure.

| Deployment type | SKU code | Billing basis | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instant (preview) | none (no deployment) | <mark>**Pay-per-token**</mark> | Prototyping, trying new models |
| Global Standard | `GlobalStandard` | <mark>**Pay-per-token**</mark> | General workloads, highest quota |
| Data Zone Standard | `DataZoneStandard` | <mark>**Pay-per-token**</mark> | EU/US data-zone compliance |
| Standard | `Standard` | <mark>**Pay-per-token**</mark> | Regional compliance, low volume |
| Global Provisioned | `GlobalProvisionedManaged` | <mark>**Reserved PTU (hourly)**</mark> | Predictable high throughput |
| Data Zone Provisioned | `DataZoneProvisionedManaged` | <mark>**Reserved PTU (hourly)**</mark> | Data zone + predictable throughput |
| Regional Provisioned | `ProvisionedManaged` | <mark>**Reserved PTU (hourly)**</mark> | Regional compliance + throughput |
| Global Batch | `GlobalBatch` | <mark>**Pay-per-token, ~50% discount, 24-hour async**</mark> | Large asynchronous jobs |
| Data Zone Batch | `DataZoneBatch` | <mark>**Pay-per-token, ~50% discount**</mark> | Async jobs with data-zone scope |
| Developer | `DeveloperTier` | <mark>**Pay-per-token, no SLA**</mark> | Fine-tuned model evaluation only |

Two billing behaviors matter most for cost analysis:

-   <mark>Standard (pay-per-token)</mark>: you pay only for tokens consumed. Cost scales directly with traffic and is easy to attribute by request, but it varies month to month.
-   <mark>Batch</mark>: the same token work runs asynchronously within a 24-hour window at roughly half the standard rate. It's the cheapest way to run large, non-interactive jobs, and it appears under its own batch meters.

## 🔒 Provisioned throughput (PTU) and reservations

<mark>**Provisioned throughput units (PTUs)**</mark> are generic units of model processing capacity. When you create a provisioned deployment, you reserve a fixed number of PTUs, and you're billed hourly for that reserved capacity — **whether or not the deployment handles any requests**.

Two characteristics define PTU billing:

1.  <mark>Billed on deployed capacity, not consumption</mark>. A <mark>300 PTU deployment</mark> is charged at `hourly rate × 300` for every hour it exists. <mark>Partial hours are prorated</mark> (15 minutes = one quarter of the hourly rate). Deployments can't be paused; billing stops only when you delete them.
2.  <mark>Model-independent quota</mark>. PTU quota is granted <mark>per region and deployment type and shared across all supported models</mark>. The same <mark>500 PTU pool can host GPT-family, DeepSeek, or Llama models</mark>. You don't buy PTUs for a specific model.

### Utilization and the output-to-input ratio

Throughput per PTU depends on the mix of input and output tokens, because output tokens cost more capacity. Each model has an <mark>output-to-input ratio</mark> used for utilization:

-   For <mark>`gpt-5`</mark>, **one** output token counts as **eight** input tokens toward utilization.
-   For <mark>`gpt-4.1`</mark>, **one** output token counts as **four** input tokens.
-   Cached tokens are **deducted 100% from utilization** — repeated prompt prefixes don't consume PTU capacity.

Utilization is reported in Azure Monitor as:

$$
\text{PTU utilization} = \frac{\text{PTUs consumed in period}}{\text{PTUs deployed in period}}
$$

When utilization passes 100%, new calls receive HTTP 429.

### Relating token throughput to PTUs

**PTUs are directly related to tokens**. The subtlety is *when* the relationship applies:

-   <mark>You pay for PTUs</mark>, not for tokens. Billing is the flat hourly capacity charge described above.
-   But <mark>you size PTUs from tokens</mark>. **A PTU is a fixed amount of token-processing** throughput, **measured in tokens per minute (TPM)**.

Each model publishes an <mark>Input TPM per PTU</mark> value — the maximum input tokens per minute that one PTU delivers. Heavier models deliver fewer TPM per PTU, so they need more PTUs for the same throughput.

| Model | Input TPM per PTU | Output-to-input ratio |
|---|---|---|
| `gpt-4.1` | <mark>**3,000**</mark> | 4 |
| `gpt-4.1-mini` | <mark>**14,900**</mark> | 4 |
| `gpt-4o` | <mark>**2,500**</mark> | tiered (see calculator) |
| `gpt-4o-mini` | <mark>**37,000**</mark> | tiered (see calculator) |
| `o4-mini` | <mark>**5,400**</mark> | tiered (see calculator) |
| `gpt-5.2` | <mark>**3,400**</mark> | 8 |

To convert an expected token workload into a PTU count, normalize the token rate (weighting output tokens by the ratio and removing cached tokens), then divide by the model's Input TPM per PTU:

$$
\text{normalized TPM} = \big(\text{input TPM} \times (1 - \text{cache rate})\big) + \big(\text{ratio} \times \text{output TPM}\big)
$$

$$
\text{PTUs required} = \left\lceil \frac{\text{normalized TPM}}{\text{Input TPM per PTU}} \right\rceil
$$

rounded up to the model's scale increment.

<mark>Worked example</mark> (Microsoft's own): `gpt-5.2` on Data Zone Provisioned, peak 1,000 requests/min, 200 input and 20 output tokens each. Input TPM per PTU = 3,400; ratio = 8.

-   Input TPM = 1,000 × 200 = 200,000
-   Output TPM = 1,000 × 20 = 20,000
-   Normalized TPM = 200,000 + (8 × 20,000) = 360,000
-   PTUs required = 360,000 ÷ 3,400 = 105.9 → **110 PTUs**
-   With a 50% cache rate: (100,000) + (8 × 20,000) = 260,000 → 76.5 → **80 PTUs**

So token volume drives *how many PTUs you provision*; once provisioned, you pay the flat hourly PTU rate no matter how many tokens you actually send. That gives a clean break-even test:

> Provisioned pays off when your *sustained* token throughput is high enough that the equivalent pay-per-token cost would exceed the flat PTU cost. Below that line, Standard (pay-per-token) is cheaper; above it, PTU — especially with a reservation — wins. Run the numbers with the [cost appendix](#-appendix-current-cost-reference-usd) and the [Azure AI Foundry capacity calculator](https://ai.azure.com/resource/calculator).

### Hourly billing versus Azure Reservations

Provisioned deployments support two billing modes:

-   <mark>Hourly billing</mark> ($/PTU/hr) for short-term needs like benchmarking or a hackathon.
-   <mark>Azure Reservations</mark> — a <mark>1-month</mark> or <mark>1-year</mark> commitment that applies a discount to the PTU meter. Reservations are purchased per deployment type (Global, Data Zone, or Regional) and scoped to <mark>subscriptions</mark> or <mark>resource groups</mark>. They're financially decoupled from deployments: create the deployment first to confirm capacity, then buy the reservation to lock in the rate.

> For the cost platform, PTU is the most important nuance: a provisioned deployment produces a *flat hourly meter* regardless of token traffic. Attribution by user or request must come entirely from telemetry, and unit economics (cost per request) depends on how well you fill the reserved capacity.

## 🎛️ Fine-tuning, caching, and modality billing

### Fine-tuned models

A fine-tuned model is billed in three separate ways:

1.  <mark>Training</mark> — charged per token in the training file, or per hour, depending on the model.
2.  <mark>Hosting</mark> — an hourly charge for the deployed fine-tuned model that applies **even when the model is idle**.
3.  <mark>Inference</mark> — per 1,000 input and output tokens when the model is called.

The hosting charge is a common source of surprise cost: a forgotten fine-tuned deployment keeps billing hourly. Remove or scale down deployments you don't need.

### Prompt caching

Prompt caching retains processed input-token computations so <mark>**identical prompt prefixes**</mark> aren't reprocessed. For supported models (o-series, GPT-4o family, GPT-4.1 family, and realtime variants), cached tokens are billed at a <mark>discount on input pricing</mark> for Standard deployments, and up to a <mark>100% discount</mark> for Provisioned deployments. Caches clear after 5–10 minutes of inactivity and never persist beyond an hour. A high cache-hit ratio is a real, measurable saving worth tracking as a platform metric.

### Modalities

The same token-metering model extends across modalities, with model-specific meters and units:

-   <mark>Text and chat</mark> — **input/output** tokens.
-   <mark>Embeddings</mark> — **input** tokens only (no generation).
-   <mark>Image generation</mark> — per image and/or per token depending on the model.
-   <mark>Audio and realtime</mark> — **token-based** metering with separate audio meters.

## 🔎 Azure AI Search billing in all its flavors

Azure AI Search is the retrieval backbone for RAG and agentic streams (and it underpins <mark>Foundry IQ</mark>, the managed knowledge layer for agents). It has two pricing models plus several premium features billed on top.

### Pricing model 1 — Dedicated (provisioned capacity)

You pre-configure capacity and pay a fixed hourly rate based on <mark>**search units (SUs)**</mark>:

$$
\text{Search units} = \text{replicas} \times \text{partitions}
$$

-   <mark>**Partitions**</mark> provide storage and indexing capacity.
-   <mark>**Replicas**</mark> provide query throughput and high availability.

A service starts at one replica and <mark>one partition</mark> (one <mark>**SU**</mark>).  
Cost scales linearly with <mark>**SUs**</mark>: adding two replicas to a one-partition service takes you from one SU to three SUs, tripling the monthly cost. Because the formula multiplies, doubling both replicas and partitions (2 × 2) creates four SUs — quadruple the cost.

Dedicated tiers, from smallest to largest:

| Tier | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| <mark>Free</mark> | Evaluation | Shared resources, no SLA, limited size |
| <mark>Basic</mark> | Small production | Up to 3 partitions / 3 replicas on new services |
| <mark>S1 / S2 / S3</mark> | Standard production | Up to 12 partitions / 12 replicas |
| <mark>S3 HD</mark> | Many small indexes | 3 partitions / 12 replicas |
| <mark>L1 / L2</mark> | Storage optimized | Large storage per partition (up to ~2 TB on L2) |

SLAs require at least two replicas for query workloads (three for query plus indexing). The number of partitions isn't an SLA factor.

### Pricing model 2 — Serverless (preview)

Serverless removes capacity planning. The service scales automatically (including to zero when idle), and you're billed on <mark>compute units (CUs)</mark> plus indexed storage (GB/month). Serverless CUs and dedicated SUs are **not** interchangeable — don't estimate one with the other's calculator.

### Premium features billed separately

Both pricing models incur additional charges for these features, each with its own billing unit:

| Feature | Billing unit | Attribution note |
|---|---|---|
| <mark>**Semantic ranker**</mark> | Per `queryType=semantic` query (non-empty search) | Progressive rate; free monthly allowance, then standard PAYG plan |
| <mark>**Agentic retrieval**</mark> | Tokens (agentic reasoning + query planning + answer formulation) | Split bill: Search bills retrieval tokens, Azure OpenAI bills planning/synthesis |
| <mark>**Image extraction (AI enrichment)**</mark> | Per 1,000 images | Text extraction is free |
| <mark>**Custom Entity Lookup skill**</mark> | Per 1,000 text records | Part of the enrichment pipeline |
| <mark>**Built-in / custom skills**</mark> | Number of transactions | Billed at the model provider's rate (Foundry or Azure-hosted) |
| <mark>**Vectorizers**</mark> | Vectorization operations | Billed at provider rate (Azure Vision, Azure OpenAI, or Foundry) |
| <mark>**Shared private link**</mark> | Bandwidth | Billed while the link exists and is used |

Two subtleties matter for accurate cost analysis:

-   <mark>Semantic ranker charges only fire</mark> when `queryType=semantic` and the search string isn't empty. A `search=*` request costs nothing even with semantic set.
-   <mark>Agentic retrieval spans two services</mark>. Search bills the retrieval and semantic-ranking tokens; the LLM query planning and answer synthesis land on your Azure OpenAI bill. From API version 2026-04-01, billing consent is separate (`semanticSearch` versus `knowledgeRetrieval`).

## 🧩 A RAG stream, decomposed by meter

A single "ask a question over my documents" RAG request can touch several meters at once. Here's how one agentic-retrieval request decomposes:

``` mermaid
flowchart TD
    U[User question] --> GW[Gateway / app]
    GW --> S[Azure AI Search]
    S -->|Search unit hours| M1[Dedicated SU meter]
    S -->|Retrieval + rerank tokens| M2[AI Search agentic meter]
    S -->|Query planning + answer synthesis tokens| M3[Azure OpenAI model meter]
    GW -->|Final generation input/output tokens| M4[Azure OpenAI model meter]
    GW -->|Optional guardrails| M5[Content Safety meter]
```

The same logical request produces:

-   A slice of the always-on <mark>Search SU hourly cost</mark> (fixed, not per request).
-   <mark>Agentic retrieval tokens</mark> on the Search bill.
-   <mark>Query planning and answer synthesis tokens</mark> on the Azure OpenAI bill.
-   <mark>Final generation tokens</mark> on the Azure OpenAI bill.
-   Optionally, <mark>Content Safety</mark> records for guardrails.

No single meter tells you "this request cost $X for this user." Only telemetry that correlates the request ID across these services can reassemble the true per-request cost — which is exactly what the platform's `fact_ai_usage` and allocation bridge are for.

## 📊 How these costs appear in Cost Management

In Azure Cost Management, group costs by **Resource** and then by **Meter** to see the breakdown:

-   <mark>**Models sold by Azure**</mark> (including Azure OpenAI) are billed directly by Microsoft and appear as model input/output meters under each Foundry resource. Meters often use the format `model-name-GUID`.
-   <mark>**Partner and community models**</mark> may appear as meters under **Global** resources (here "Global" refers to the billing grouping, not the deployment SKU).
-   With multiple Foundry resources, your bill contains one entry per model per resource.
-   <mark>**Azure AI Search**</mark> appears as its tier/SU meter plus separate meters for semantic ranker, agentic retrieval, and enrichment.
-   <mark>**PTU deployments**</mark> appear as flat hourly capacity meters; reservations show as a separate reservation charge and discount.

HTTP status codes don't determine billing — charges depend on whether billable processing occurred. Always treat the invoice and meter records as the source of truth, and reconcile telemetry against them.

## 🧱 What this means for attribution

Mapping the billing mechanics to the platform's medallion model:

| Billing fact | Lands in | Attribution source needed |
|---|---|---|
| Model token meters (Standard) | `fact_cloud_cost` | Gateway token telemetry per request/user |
| PTU hourly meters | `fact_cloud_cost` | Telemetry proportions to spread flat cost across users/requests |
| Fine-tuning hosting hourly | `fact_cloud_cost` | Owning application/team tag |
| Search SU hourly | `fact_cloud_cost` | Consuming workload weighting |
| Semantic ranker / agentic tokens | `fact_cloud_cost` | Request-level query telemetry |

The recurring pattern: <mark>pay-per-token meters are the easiest to attribute</mark> (usage is proportional to cost), while <mark>reserved-capacity meters (PTU, Search SUs, fine-tuning hosting) are flat</mark> and must be spread across consumers using telemetry weights inside each Cost Management bucket.

## ✅ Key takeaways

-   Foundry bills models by **tokens** (input, output, cached), with the deployment type deciding whether you pay per token or per reserved PTU hour.
-   **PTU and reservations** bill flat hourly capacity, independent of traffic — great for predictable load, but attribution and unit economics depend on utilization.
-   **Fine-tuning** adds a persistent hourly hosting charge on top of training and inference.
-   **Azure AI Search** bills dedicated capacity by search units (replicas × partitions) or serverless by compute units, with **semantic ranker, agentic retrieval, and AI enrichment billed separately** — and agentic retrieval spans both the Search and Azure OpenAI bills.
-   A single RAG request fans out across multiple meters, so per-request cost only exists once telemetry stitches those meters back together.
